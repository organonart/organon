## Question

This codebase is opinionated and says so at length. Nearly every constraint in it comes
with a written justification: the plugin identity that must never change, the append-only
IPC snapshot, the licence split across crates, the three editions selected at compile
time, the two-process split between plugin and visual, the doc-enforcement hooks.

**Sort those constraints into three piles**, and justify each placement from the code:

1. **Load-bearing** — something outside the repository depends on it. Changing it breaks
   a saved DAW session, a running process, an existing install. The written reason and
   the real reason are the same.
2. **Inherited** — a reasonable decision, once, whose original cause has weakened or gone.
   It is now maintained by momentum and by the document that explains it.
3. **Accidental** — never decided, just accreted, and now defended in retrospect.

The documents will tell you which pile they think each one is in. Your job is to check
that, not to repeat it. Where a justification is **circular** — the constraint is
justified by a document that exists because of the constraint — say so.

Then, three forward questions:

- **What breaks at the next increment?** Name the specific structures that will not
  survive another year of the growth the commit log shows, and say what the failure looks
  like — not "it will get complex", but which file, which boundary, which invariant.
- **Where is the cost concentrated?** Point to the specific places a change is expensive,
  and say whether the expense buys anything.
- **What would you not change?** Name the parts that are genuinely well-made. A critique
  that finds nothing to keep has not understood what it is reading.

## Scope

Inside the repository. `ARCHITECTURE.md` is the entry point and is large — read its file
map and its extension-point sections rather than the whole thing, then go to the code.
`doc/arch/topology.md` owns the crate graph, `doc/arch/render.md` the render pipeline,
`MIND_ARCHITECTURE.md` and `SHELL_ARCHITECTURE.md` their products' living state.

`native/tools/crate-churn.py` measures how often a commit crosses crate boundaries and
exists precisely to settle one of these arguments with a number rather than an opinion.
Its output is evidence; use it.

Out of scope: documentation drift (`doc-code-fidelity` owns it), the market
(`product-landscape` owns it), and style. Do not report formatting, naming or line length
— the repository is generated by subtraction from a private monorepo and is byte-identical
to its upstream, which makes tidying patches actively harmful.

## Method

Read for **seams**, not for lines. The questions that pay:

- What has to change together? Trace one real addition end to end — a parameter is
  documented as a seven-file chain — and count what a contributor must hold in their head.
- Which structures are large because the problem is large, and which are large because
  nobody split them? The repository publishes its own worst offenders on every session
  start (one function past seven thousand lines, one struct past a thousand fields) and
  argues that length is not the metric while field count is. Test that argument.
- Where does a compile-time switch stand in for a runtime abstraction, and what does that
  cost? Three editions are selected by cargo feature, and two of them are default-off,
  so the default build does not compile them.
- Which invariants are enforced by a machine, and which by a paragraph? Count both. A
  paragraph-enforced invariant in a repository worked on by AI agents is a different risk
  than one enforced by a test.

Prefer a claim you can anchor to a file over a claim you can only phrase well.

## Deliverable

Beyond the standard output contract, include:

- The three-pile table: constraint · pile · the evidence that put it there · what the
  documents claim.
- A section **"The next thing that breaks"** — ranked, with the specific structure named.
- A section **"What is genuinely well-made here"** — specific, and not a courtesy.

Mark structural claims `verified` only when you traced them in the code. Everything
predictive is `speculative`, even when it is obvious; the value of this brief a year from
now is being able to check which predictions came true, and that only works if the
predictions are labelled as such.
